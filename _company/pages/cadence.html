Cadence

Real-time AI modeled after animal and human brains.

Cadence learns from live experience. It senses, remembers, decides and acts as one continuous brain, and it keeps changing with what happens to it.

The difference

A new kind of AI.

Most of today's AI runs on transformers. Cadence follows the design of a brain.

Transformers

Trained first, then deployed frozen.

A transformer is completely feed-forward: each output is computed in one pass from input to output. In a typical deployed model the attention matrices and all other weights are fixed once training ends. What the model meets in use does not change them. New knowledge means fine-tuning or retraining, a separate run that usually happens in a data centre.

Cadence

Running and learning at once.

Signals loop through the brain until its parts agree on an answer. Each action and its result changes the connections involved. The brain that works is the brain that learns, in real time, for its whole life.

Why Cadence

Built like a brain.
Made for a body.

A robot meets wear, new loads and places nobody trained it for. A brain that learns from experience can keep up with them.

On the job

It learns while it works.

The same brain acts and learns from the result of every action. There is no training phase before deployment.

Changed conditions

It adapts when things change.

A worn motor, a heavier load or a different floor shows up in what the robot senses. The brain adjusts from that, without being told what changed.

On the machine

It learns on the robot itself.

Each connection changes based on the activity next to it, as in a biological brain. Learning needs no round trip to a data centre.

Memory

It remembers.

A short-term memory carries the last few moments and a long-term memory keeps what mattered. Something experienced once can shape the next decision.

Settled answers

It does not act on a guess.

Cadence acts only once the whole brain has settled on an answer. When it cannot settle, it reports that, and the robot can fall back to a safe behaviour.

Inspection

You can see what it is doing.

The activity of every part of the brain can be read while it runs. The brain can also try out a response in imagination before the body commits to it.

Shown in simulation today in Rover Lab, Patch World and the Atari Arcade. Cadence is experimental research software, and a physical robot evaluation is the next test.

How it works

Many small parts.
One answer.

A brain has no central processor. Each cell does a small local job, and a decision is what the cells agree on. Cadence works on the same principle.

01

Small parts that read themselves.

Cadence is built from small units called patches. Each patch holds its own state, listens to its neighbours, keeps a record of experience and checks its own predictions against what happens.

02

They settle on one answer.

The patches correct their disagreements with each other until the whole brain settles. The settled state is the decision. This runs continuously, in real time.

03

Experience changes the connections.

After each action the result feeds back into the brain. Each connection adjusts a little, using only what it can see locally. The brain learns while it runs.

An animal-like brain, with optional reflection.

The default brain perceives, remembers, acts and learns, as an animal's does. We call it System 1. An optional System 2 adds parts that observe the brain's own activity and feed back into it.

System 1 and System 2 name roles in software. Cadence takes its design principles from brains and makes no claim to copy biology in detail.

Compared with deep learning

Deep, without backpropagation.

A Cadence brain can have as many layers as a deep neural network. What differs is how an answer forms and how the brain learns.

A deep neural network

One pass forward. Errors passed back.

Answering. A signal passes through the layers once, from input to output. A later layer never changes an earlier one.

Learning. Training uses backpropagation: an error measured at the output is carried backwards through every layer. It happens in a training phase that is separate from use.

Depth. More layers make both passes longer.

A Cadence brain

The whole brain settles.

Answering. Signals run in both directions. Each patch corrects its disagreement with its neighbours until the whole brain reaches an equilibrium. That settled state is the answer.

Learning. The result of an action disturbs the equilibrium. The patches repair the disagreement locally, the connections involved adjust, and the brain settles into a new equilibrium. There is no separate backward pass.

Depth. More layers join the same settlement, and every connection keeps learning from the activity next to it.

What changes during settlement and learning?

For a query, weights and biases stay fixed while internal states settle. When Cadence admits an experience, supplied outcomes can constrain outputs and the relation parameters can change too, anchored to their values before that experience. Both layouts use that learning API; this illustration keeps parameters fixed.

State connections let a later constraint revise an earlier state. Observers also bring exact prediction errors into that interaction. Returning arrows show influence through the objective's derivatives, not additional learned reverse edges. Changing the sensory signal retains activity; changing the layout resets it.

Observer depth, repair steps and prediction into the future are three different things.

The patch equations behind the illustration

predictionᵢ = tanh(biasᵢ + Σ weightᵢⱼ × signalⱼ)
errorᵢ = stateᵢ − predictionᵢ
objective = ½ Σ errorᵢ² + 0.18/2 × Σ stateᵢ²

A signal can be a supplied input, another patch's state or an observed prediction error. Predictions and errors are recomputed from the current state; errors cannot be adjusted independently to conceal disagreement.

The illustration uses bounded state updates, exact derivatives through the error readbacks and backtracking for objective decrease. It stops at its stationarity tolerance or a 240-step budget. The objective's numerical “energy” does not measure electrical energy.

The positive activity penalty matters. For the corresponding acyclic, unclamped model in the idealized zero-penalty limit, the stationary answer reduces to forward substitution. Positive penalties or output constraints allow a different compromise. That difference alone does not establish better task performance.

How this differs from backpropagation

A conventional network is trained by a backward pass that carries errors through every layer, usually offline over a dataset, and is then frozen for use. In Cadence's default brain each synapse compares the activity of its two neurons in two settled states, one free and one nudged by the outcome, and changes by their difference. Reward scales that change, and memory writes are local too. The brain keeps no backward computation graph.

Under idealized conditions this local contrast follows the gradient that backpropagation would compute. Networks trained by backpropagation can also be recurrent, online and memory-using; the comparison is of update mechanisms. What the brain acquires is measured through its free behavior.

Advanced engines, capability and cost

Cadence 0.71.0 keeps specialized engines beside the main brain, each with its own contract. In the population solver, populations of patches settle one joint energy and observers read exact prediction errors; the interactive model above shows its equations. Record patches hold event records and consolidate them. A temporal model learns the consequences of actions and plans with them. Start with the smallest brain that learns the behavior, then measure what observers add. Measure query latency and learning cost separately.
